There's an anecdote early in the essay about a commuter who describes his finance job in a flat monotone, then lights up when he pulls out a half-finished knitted hat. He made it, he explains, "to do something."

Aaron Horwath's ["Why Is Everyone In Tech So Sad?"](https://www.noemamag.com/why-is-everyone-in-tech-so-sad/) ran in Noema last week. The [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49209539) about it collected over 1,300 comments, and the moderators renamed it to "What happens if an entire class of workers loses faith in their careers," which is somehow even bleaker than the original title.

## What the essay argues

Horwath builds on Derek Thompson's idea of Workism: educated professionals stopped going to church and started going to work for the same reasons. The job became the place where identity and purpose were supposed to come from.

That faith survived even in obviously pointless jobs, he argues, because the work still involved humans. You brainstormed with people, argued with people, shipped things with people. The effort itself felt like proof that the work mattered.

Then agents arrived. When a strategy deck or a codebase materializes in minutes without the human middle, the question "was any of this ever necessary?" stops being philosophical. The busyness used to hide the emptiness, and AI removed the busyness.

He also splits knowledge workers into two groups. Outcome-first people care about the thing getting built and treat the process as overhead. Experience-first people, the craftsmen, get their meaning from the process itself. AI is a gift to the first group and a slow grief for the second.

## What Hacker News made of it

Three camps, roughly.

The craftsmen wrote the comments that stay with you. One engineer with twenty years in the industry and about sixty programming books on his shelf described a year of prompting Claude: "Despite having built more in the last year than would have ever been possible before, I feel empty. The outcomes work, they work well, but I didn't write the code." Another described the mood in the industry as "ABSOLUTELY LEGENDARY amounts of cope."

The second camp rejected the whole framing. "I don't need faith, I need a paycheck" was the shortest version. For them the sadness has nothing to do with philosophy. It's layoffs, a frozen hiring market, and entry-level roles disappearing. Essays about meaning are a luxury of the still-employed.

The third camp enjoyed the irony. "Programmers should be happy. Their entire career has revolved around automating everything and replacing people with tech. I'm really not sure what the big deal is?" Fair hit. And buried in the thread was a quieter report from someone who had actually lived the escape fantasy, sold everything and traveled: "It's fun for 3-6 months. But it's only fun - not an existential cure. That could only be found in friends/loved ones."

## Where I stand

The essay is right, and the thread is the evidence. You don't get 1,300 comments of grief about a philosophy essay unless the faith it describes is real and cracking.

If anything, the conclusion lands softer than it should. Work-as-religion was the bug all along, and AI is just the moment the lights came on. If your sense of meaning evaporates because a model can now do your tasks, the meaning was stored in the wrong place. It lived in the ritual, in the typing and the feeling of being needed. The ritual turned out to be automatable, and the reason you started never was.

I began coding at 13, running game servers and selling things online, with no salary and no title, and with my mom turning off the PC when I played too long. The point was that something existed in the evening that hadn't existed in the morning. Seventeen years later that's still the whole deal for me. Code was always the instrument, never the meaning.

## The website you're reading

This site is my test case. Most of it was built by agents. There's a [dashboard on the homepage](/#stats) that measures it: cache reads, the model re-reading its own context, make up 97% of the tokens, and the share where Claude actually writes something is 0.3%. That 0.3% is still far more of this site's code than I typed by hand. My contribution is taste and the occasional veto.

![My site's dashboard showing the token ratio: 97% of tokens are re-reading context, 0.3% is Claude actually writing](/images/thoughts/why-is-everyone-in-tech-so-sad/token-ratio.jpg)

*Live from the [dashboard](/#stats). Claude reads 97% of the time and writes 0.3% of the time, which sounds familiar to anyone who has worked with consultants.*

By the essay's taxonomy that makes me outcome-first, one of the people the craftsmen resent. But shipping this site didn't feel like operating a vending machine. Deciding what it says and what gets cut felt exactly like the thing I've been chasing since the game servers, just with a stranger brush.

The craftsmen in that thread have my sympathy. Losing the part of the job you loved is a real loss, and no blog post talks anyone out of grief. I just notice that the commuter from the essay didn't wait for anyone to hand meaning back to him. He bought yarn.
